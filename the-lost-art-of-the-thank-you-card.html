The Lost Art of the Thank You Card

Livinggentlemanetiquette

Does anyone still write a thank you card?

I sat down the other day to write some, finally, for a family trip from last year. I did not have anyone's mailing address. So I asked. I said I'd rather send a proper card late than let it go entirely, and that I had already let it go longer than I meant to.

The addresses came back. No lecture. Just the list.

That is how the art gets lost. Not with a speech about manners dying. It gets lost because the note starts to feel like a chore, and a chore is easy to put off, and putting it off starts to feel like the polite option. Don't bother them. Don't be late. Don't make it weird. And then you never send it.

Thanks, congratulations, condolences. A gentleman does not put those in the chore pile. He writes them because they cost almost nothing and they tell someone you noticed them. The people you respect. The people you owe. The people you actually like being around.

That is the whole argument. The stamp costs less than a coffee. The card takes ten minutes if you stop trying to write a speech. What it carries is the fact that you sat down and thought about them on purpose.

A text disappears by morning. A card sits on a counter. Somebody puts it on the fridge. Years later you find one in a drawer and you remember who sent it.

On a Tuesday it looks like this:

  • Keep a small stack of blank cards and stamps where you actually sit, not in a box in the closet.
  • Three sentences is enough. What they did. What it meant. That you are glad they are in your life, or sorry for their loss, or proud of them.
  • Send it late rather than not at all.

On a Monday it looked like this. My own cards had just come in, "From the Desk of" across the top, and I had two to mail.

The first was a retirement note to a colleague I have taught alongside for a long time. One stamp, into the box at the post office, done.

The second went to a man in Houston I met through a gentlemen's group. He keeps embossed stationery on his desk and still likes a wax seal, so I sealed it. Then I put the whole thing in a padded mailer, because I have had a seal come out of a sorting machine in pieces.

The plain card cost one stamp. The sealed one cost eleven dollars and change. Same post office, same afternoon. One went first class and the other went with an armed escort.

Both of them will be sitting on a counter somewhere this week.

The addresses made the late version possible. The card is still mine to write.
